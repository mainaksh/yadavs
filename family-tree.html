गोत्र प्रणाली

The 84 Gotras — Sage-Lineage Groups

The Yadava community is organised into 84+ exogamous gotra groups, each named after a Vedic sage (rishi) from whom the lineage claims patrilineal descent. Marriage within the same gotra is prohibited — the system has maintained genetic and social diversity across five thousand years. The gotras are the community's deepest genealogical memory.

Krishnatreya
कृष्णात्रेय

वंश-काल रेखा

The Dynastic Timeline — From Cosmic Origin to Today

The Yadava genealogy is not merely horizontal (who begat whom) but vertical — spanning six thousand years of documented or traditionally accepted history. This timeline anchors the family tree in historical time.

Vedic Period · Before 3100 BCE (traditional)
Brahma → Atri → Chandra → Pururavas — The Cosmic Root
The Yadava genealogy begins in the Rigveda's cosmology — with Brahma as the ultimate progenitor, Atri as the Saptarishi whose son Chandra (the Moon) founds the Chandravamsha (Lunar Dynasty). Pururavas, the Moon's grandson, begins the royal line. These figures are pan-Hindu; their significance is that the Yadava genealogy is consciously anchored to the oldest layer of Indian cosmological thought.
Epic Period · c.3100–1500 BCE (traditional)
Yayati → Yadu — The Founding Separation
King Yayati has five sons — Yadu by Devayani and Puru by Sharmishtha. Yayati curses Yadu for refusing to exchange youth, but simultaneously establishes the Yadu line as a parallel dynasty. Yadu's descendants — the Yadavas — become the premier pastoral-warrior community of north-west India. Yadu's five sons found five sub-lineages, of which the Vrishni line (through the third son) becomes paramount.
Late Vedic · c.1500–800 BCE
The Vrishni Lineage — Devamidha, Shura, Vasudeva
The Vrishni sub-clan of the Yadava confederation establishes itself at Mathura in the Braj region. Through Devamidha → Shura → Vasudeva, the lineage reaches the generation of Krishna's father. Vasudeva is a chieftain of the Vrishni; his son Krishna will be simultaneously a Yadava king and the most significant figure in all of Indian religious and cultural history.
Mahabharata Era · c.3100 BCE traditional / c.800 BCE scholarly
Krishna — The Divine Centre of the Yadava Tree
Krishna Vasudeva — warrior, king, philosopher, cowherd, divine ancestor — occupies the unique position in the Yadava genealogy of being simultaneously a historical figure within the lineage and the divine absolute whose birth within the lineage sanctifies the entire family tree retrospectively and prospectively. The Bhagavad Gita, taught to Arjuna on the Kurukshetra battlefield, is the world's most influential philosophical text produced within this lineage.
Classical Period · 300 BCE – 600 CE
Post-Mahabharata Dispersal — The Sub-Communities Form
Following the Kurukshetra war and the submersion of Dvaraka, the Yadava community disperses across the Indian subcontinent. The Ahir, Gop, Gwala, Golla, Kuruba, Idaiyar, and Gaoli sub-communities solidify their distinct regional identities while maintaining the common thread of pastoral economy and Krishna devotion. Regional inscriptions (particularly the Abhira inscriptions of central India, 2nd–4th century CE) provide the first epigraphic evidence of these communities.
Medieval Period · 600–1300 CE
Yadava Kingdom of Devagiri — Peak Political Power
The Yadava dynasty of Devagiri (present-day Daulatabad, Maharashtra) rules as the premier power of the Deccan from approximately 850 to 1317 CE. Under kings like Bhillama V, Singhana, and Ramachandra, the kingdom patronises Marathi language (the first Marathi literary works emerge under Yadava patronage), maintains a powerful military, and controls trade routes between North India and the Konkan coast. The dynasty ends with Ramachandra's submission to Alauddin Khalji in 1296 and the subsequent Khalji conquest.
Bhakti Period · 1300–1700 CE
Surdas, Mirabai, Raidas — The Bhakti Flowering
The Bhakti movement produces the community's greatest cultural flowering — Surdas's Sur Sagar (800,000 verses of Krishna devotional poetry in Braj Bhasha), Mirabai's devotional lyrics (the most beloved female poet in all of Indian literature), Raidas's social-justice Bhakti (first Dalit saint of the Krishna tradition), and the entire Braj literary tradition centred on Mathura-Vrindavana. The community's devotional poetry in this period constitutes the apex of the entire Yadava cultural contribution to human civilization.
Colonial Period · 1700–1947 CE
Rao Tula Ram, Mandal Commission — The Political Awakening
Under colonial rule, the Yadava community — classified as an "agricultural and pastoral caste" in British administrative categories — begins organising politically. Rao Tula Ram (1825–1863) leads the 1857 uprising in Haryana; B.P. Mandal (1918–1982) rises to Bihar CM and chairs the commission that will transform Indian democracy. The colonial period also produces the first documented community organisations (Yadava Mahasabha, 1920s) and the first self-conscious assertions of Yadava political identity.
Post-Mandal Era · 1990 – Present
350 Million — The Living Tree's Present Moment
The Mandal revolution (1990) converts ancestral social cohesion into political power — Lalu Prasad Yadav and Mulayam Singh Yadav become Chief Ministers of India's two most populous states. Bihar's 2023 caste survey officially records Yadavas as 14.26% of Bihar's population. The community today encompasses 350 million people across India and the global diaspora, distributed across 1,200+ sub-communities, united by the ancestral pastoral tradition, Krishna devotion, and the long memory of five thousand years of continuous civilisational identity.
